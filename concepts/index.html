    How NAVE works

    NAVE explains a frozen vision encoder: it extracts concepts from the encoder's feature activations at several depths and maps them back onto the image. The explanation does not depend on a classifier or a label.

    Layers and composite pixels

    An encoder is a stack of layers (blocks). Each outputs a feature map: a grid of positions, each with a vector of C numbers. A ResNet's four blocks halve the resolution each time:

    Block Stride Grid at 448 px Channels (ResNet18 / 50)
    1 4 112 x 112 64 / 256
    2 8 56 x 56 128 / 512
    3 16 28 x 28 256 / 1024
    4 32 14 x 14 512 / 2048

    ViT-Ti/16 and ViT-S/16 have 12 blocks, all on the same grid of 16 px patches (192 and 384 channels).

    NAVE (Algorithm 1 of the paper) combines the chosen layers per position:

    1. Upsample every chosen layer to the grid of the shallowest one.
    2. Normalise each position's vector to length 1 (normalize=True).
    3. Scale each layer by C_min / (1 + C_b), so layers with many channels do not dominate (scale=True).
    4. Concatenate: each position becomes a composite pixel mixing detail and semantics.
    5. Project the composite pixels onto K concepts.

    The paper's notation names the layers: N34 is blocks 3 and 4; N:9-12 is blocks 9 and 12.

    For the built-in ViTs, features="keys" reads the attention keys of the chosen blocks instead of their outputs: each block's input after its layer norm, through the key projection, before attention mixes the tokens. This is what LOST and TokenCut use for object discovery with DINO. Saliency and class predictions still use the blocks' outputs, which the classifier reads. normalize=False and scale=False reproduce the paper's ablations.

    Projectors

    projector=
    None / "kmeans" k-means++ seeding and Lloyd iterations; prototypes are the centroids
    "bayesian" k-means with a Dirichlet prior on the weights, by Gibbs sampling; k is an upper bound
    "pca" principal components; a pixel goes to the component with the largest absolute projection
    any object scikit-learn style: clusterers (predict + transform, predict_proba, or fit_predict) or projections (transform only: PCA, NMF, ICA)

    Bayesian k-means is a finite approximation of a Dirichlet-process mixture: π ~ Dirichlet(α/K), z ~ Categorical(π), x ~ N(μ_z, σ²I) with one shared σ². Assignments are sampled with the cluster means integrated out, leaving each point out of its own cluster, so duplicate clusters merge and the number of concepts used is learned. After bayes_burn_in sweeps, posterior means are averaged and pixels assigned with them; concepts below 0.5% of the posterior weight are dropped. It runs on sample_size composite pixels.

    From the grid to pixels

    Concepts are extracted on the feature grid, coarser than the image. nave-xai interpolates the concept scores to pixel resolution and takes the best concept per pixel: the explanation map. With upsample="nearest" this gives the paper's blocky maps; "linear" and "cubic" smooth boundaries. One concept can appear in several places; segmentation() gives the connected regions.

    Uncertainty

    With uncertainty=True, every pixel gets H(softmax(scores / T)) / log K, the normalised entropy of its concept assignment, from 0 (one concept clearly wins) to 1 (all equally likely). For k-means, scores are minus the squared distances to the prototypes, so 1 - uncertainty = 1 - H(softmax(-d² / T)) / log K is the certainty. The temperature T is uncertainty_temp, 0.1 by default; "auto" uses T = 1 for probabilistic projectors and otherwise the median gap between the two best concept scores.

    Ordering and colours

    After fitting, concepts are renumbered along a dendrogram of their prototypes (reorder="ward" in fit). Ward linkage is exact from prototypes and concept sizes: the cost of merging two clusters, n_a n_b / (n_a + n_b) |c_a - c_b|², depends only on their centroids and sizes. "average" and "single" linkage use distances between prototypes. With groups, concepts are ordered by group first, and every group gets a colour family.

    reorder(by="classes") groups concepts under the class they correlate with most (from correlation), unassigned ones last; recolor=True gives every class a colour and its concepts shades of it (color_vary, color_spread).

    Patches and correlation

    A cell is a connected region of an explanation map assigned to one concept. For every concept, the closest cell (smallest mean distance of its composite pixels to the prototype) and the largest cell of the fitting set are kept as patches: 32 x 32 views, dimmed outside the cell, with a 1 px white line around it. correlation is the Pearson correlation, across images, between a concept's proportion in an image and the image's class (the groups given to fit, else the model's prediction).

    Concept-based saliency

    saliency(image) explains a class with the encoder's own regions:

    1. At every position of the deepest chosen layer (before pooling), take the cosine between the feature vector and the classifier's weight vector of the target class.
    2. Explain that layer alone with NAVE and average the cosine over each region.
    3. Move one layer shallower: upsample, explain with the cumulative layers (N4, then N34, then N234) and average per region again.
    4. Upsample the result to the image.
